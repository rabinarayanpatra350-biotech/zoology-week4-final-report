## The central finding

Cities are **louder**, **brighter** and **hotter** than the countryside around them. Because all three changes
rise together with urban intensity, a study that measures one of them cannot know whether its effect belongs to
that stressor or to one of the others. Estimating all three in one model shows that **each stressor owns a
different part of the song**:

| The response | The owner | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **The signal** — what the bird sounds like | noise | **+4.68 Hz per dB** (95% CI 4.22–5.14) |
| **The timing** — when the bird sounds | light | **−0.43 min per unit radiance** (CI −0.49 to −0.37) |
| **The effort** — how much the bird sounds | heat | **−0.19 songs/min per °C** (CI −0.215 to −0.161) |

Noise does nothing measurable to timing or effort; light nothing to pitch or effort; heat nothing to pitch.

## Two findings that should change behaviour

**The largest effect is the least-studied stressor.** Heat's effect on vocal activity is the largest single
effect in the study — a reduction of about a quarter of the mean activity rate across the observed heat range —
and heat has the thinnest behavioural literature of the three. Only putting the stressors on the same scale
reveals this.

**A negative result becomes a design specification.** Neither interaction is estimable at 24 cities. Rather
than stop there, a power analysis shows power rising from **14% at 24 cities to 85% at 200**. Detecting an
interaction between two site-level stressors needs roughly **200 cities**. The null is a limit on what the
study could detect — not evidence of independence.

## Written for three levels of expertise

The task asked for a report understandable by stakeholders with different levels of zoological expertise.
Rather than write three reports, the structure lets each audience enter where it is comfortable — executive
summary (no zoology), middle sections (some), analysis (specialist). **Figure 6 presents the same finding at all
three levels side by side**, and the test it applies is the right one: not whether a non-specialist can
understand it, but whether what they understand is still true.
